# 3 Review
Ok, as a whole it is hard to dislike this book. It is, at its core, a plea to readers to care about a disease that doesn’t directly affect them. And the point is well made, very well made. But i still can't give this more than a 3.5/5 because of the writing style. It's not bad at the level that it's infuriating, but it did make me question some editorial choices. That said, there's enough this made me think. I think I've always been emotional about some of the themes in this book, and this gave me a few more lenses to look at a few things.
## 3.1 The writing
I think the book has a really strong start and a very fulfilling ending. It's somewhere in the middle that i found Greene regurgitating the point he wanted to make. 

When the book starts, it got me really hooked by some stories from history weirdly connected to TB in some way, for instance the World war 1 story was fascinating. This got me excited because i thought Greene has chosen a rather creative way to bring our attention to this topic by showing how so many aspects of our lives is influenced by this 1 disease and our reaction to the disease. Sadly though, later on, the book does not deliver on it's title this way. A more appropriate title would "A brief history of Tb".

The choice to introduce Henry as a means to tie facts with story also seemed like a genius way to write a non-fiction. But, it seemed he forgot about Henry's story right through the middle, and only brings him back in the final few chapters. Not that he was required to do so, but it got me less invested into that part of the story, and made it feel like a rather confused way of writing. At some point he gives the Marco-Polo analogy, and i believe this would've made me cry on a normal day, if it was written in a nice flow.

The point that is repeated too often is society's reaction to the disease and how the stigma caused the cure not to reach the patients. To be fair, this is the point Greene wants to make. But hear me out. At some point, he talks about J&J(a substution for capitalism) holding off on patents and not allowing the cure to reach everyone. I wish the author had done more of this, and not be mostly vague by assigning the blame of society as a whole. I wanted to know who was making these decisions and why. And also why TB is different to other communicable diseases.

The ending, especially the postscript does bring it together though, and next section starts with that.

## 3.2 Made me think
“The problem with statistics is that I cannot take in what it means to lose 1,250,000 people each year to a curable illness. That’s more than a hundred thousand people a month. But how do I conceptualise such statistics? I’ve been in a stadium with a hundred thousand people, but I didn’t know each of their families. I didn’t know about the people they’ve loved, the heartbreaks they’ve endured, their constraints and encouragements, their frailty and resilience. I simply cannot fathom what 1,250,000 means.”
Pretty self-explanatory this one. History is always brutal in the way that people are reduced to numbers looking back. But it's only natural and probably a motivation to move on. The idea that by picking any one event in history and analysing the stories of every person attached to it could give us a few million stories about a speck in time, is fascinating to me. With disease and death, it's sad, but fascinating nonetheless.

The idea of glorifying the disease because of how it made people look, showed me how stupid as a species we can sometimes be. I think we have a natural tendency to assume correlation equals causation. I wonder why we do not, it's definitely not scientific, but maybe it is logical, and that is how humankind has discovered what it has about the world

Do we need more proof that God, in the way we want him to exist, does not in fact exist. We're at the peak of human civilisation because a select few were willing to let go of faith and fall back to logic. When has praying or mass sacrifices saved as many people as modern medicine? And what's ironic is that the gatekeepers of religion use modern medicine in the very way they're born and not contract diseases as they go along. Its convenient to argue for the other side without any proof, while using the very thing you're arguing against
